# Week 21 — Challenges

The exercises drill the mechanics. **The challenge makes you the security engineer** reviewing a policy that *looks* zero-trust — it has SPIRE identities, it has an OPA policy, the deny-by-default line is right there at the top — and yet a compromised pod can reach the payment service it should never touch. The policy passed review because everyone saw `default allow := false` and stopped reading. The hole is one over-broad rule below it, and it's the exact bug that turns a single foothold into a full breach. This is the most common real zero-trust failure: not the *absence* of a policy, but a policy that *authorizes more than it should.*

## Index

1. **[Challenge 1 — The over-broad policy](challenge-01-the-over-broad-policy.md)** — a Rego authorization policy that has all the zero-trust trappings (SPIFFE identities, deny-by-default) but contains an over-broad allow rule (a wildcard, or a too-loose match) that silently authorizes lateral movement to `payment`. Using `opa test`, `opa eval`, and the allow-matrix, you must (a) prove a workload that should be denied is actually allowed, (b) find and name the over-broad rule that opened the hole, and (c) fix it to genuine least privilege — *without* breaking the legitimate call paths, and with a test that would have caught it. (~90 min)

Challenges are optional for passing the week, but this one is the single most important zero-trust lesson there is. A *missing* policy is obvious — there's no authorization at all. An *over-broad* policy is the dangerous one, because it has every appearance of security (the deny-by-default, the SPIFFE identities, the green "we have OPA" checkbox) while quietly granting the lateral movement that zero trust exists to prevent. The engineer who can read a policy that "looks fine," write the test that proves `cart` can reach `payment` when it shouldn't, and tighten the rule to true least privilege, is the one who makes zero trust actually zero-trust instead of security theater with extra YAML.
